Features

  • Makes a V8 tach read rightLS controllers put out 2 pulses per revolution; a V8 tach expects 4. Hob doubles the signal so the tach reads correctly without retuning the PCM or reconfiguring the gauge.
  • Protects your PCMSeries resistance and a Schottky clamp guard the input, and each output has its own driver. A short on a dash wire hits Hob, not your PCM.
  • Ignores noiseThe Schmitt-trigger input has a dead band between its thresholds, so ignition noise doesn't turn into false pulses or dropouts. No more jumping needle.
  • Easy on the PCMHob asks about 0.6 mA of the PCM's tach pin per pulse. The common 1k pull-up resistor asks about 14 mA, from an output whose ratings you can't look up.
  • Two outputsRun your tach on one and a shift light or other RPM-based device on the other, each with its own driver.
  • Check it at a glanceThe LED glows dim with power and brighter when pulses arrive, so you can confirm signal under the hood without a meter.

About Hob

LS swaps break tach signals in predictable ways: the pulse count doesn't match what a V8 gauge expects, the signal is too weak to register, or it's noisy enough to make the needle jump around. The usual fix is a pull-up resistor. It moves the needle, but it leaves the signal problems in place and puts the PCM's output pin straight onto the harness with nothing in between.

Hob is the real fix: an active conditioner that takes the weak LS signal in and puts out a clean, correctly scaled one your tach can trust. First prototype boards are in hand and waiting on bench testing; we'll post bench measurements once testing is done.

A hob, short for hobgoblin, is a household spirit from British folklore that quietly tends the hearth and keeps a home running right, so long as it's treated with respect. Get on its bad side and it turns mischievous. Fitting, for a device whose whole job is to take a misbehaving signal and quietly put it right.
